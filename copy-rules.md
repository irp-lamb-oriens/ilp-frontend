# ILP copy rules

Every word a visitor, member, lawyer or staff member reads follows these rules: landing pages, forms, notices, tutorials, emails and dashboard text. Agents and people editing copy read this file first.

## Voice

Plain, specific, calm. Write for a North American aged 40 to 75 who knows nothing about the law of Colombia, Costa Rica, Mexico, Panama, Portugal or Spain, about ILP or about software. Say what a thing is, what will happen, and why it matters to the reader, in that order of usefulness. Prefer concrete facts from the product (US$79.99 a month, three 20-minute calls, 60 minutes, a lawyer licensed in the member's country) over adjectives. Vary sentence length: mix short sentences with longer ones that carry detail. Never invent facts, numbers, testimonials, partners or results.

## Banned patterns (the AI giveaways)

1. Rhetorical questions you answer yourself ("Want to sleep better?").
2. Elliptical setups and dramatic pauses ("The best part... you don't lift a finger").
3. Suspense question plus short answer ("Is it easy? Absolutely.").
4. Sincerity fillers: honestly, frankly, to be honest, genuinely, truly, actually.
5. Revelation hooks: "the secret nobody tells you", "here's the truth", "scary truth".
6. Overhype: life-changing, game-changer, revolutionary, effortless, seamless, unlock, supercharge.
7. "Quietly" used to sound like insider knowledge.
8. "Here's the kicker", "the crazy thing", "the best part".
9. "Point AI at X" and other vague delegation shorthand.
10. "X will revolutionize Y".
11. "In today's fast-changing world" and other global-context openers.
12. "Dive into", "let's explore", "let's break it down", "without further ado", "here's what you need to know".
13. Big contrasts and ultimatums ("adapt or get left behind").
14. Opposing shutdowns ("Forget everything you know about lawyers").
15. Three adjectives in a row ("fast, simple, secure").
16. "No X, no Y, just Z".
17. Forced groups of three. Use as many items as are true: one, two, four.
18. Reframes: "You're not buying X, you're buying Y", "not to X but to Y", "X isn't Y, it's Z".
19. Philosophical reductions and aphorisms ("Peace of mind is the real currency").
20. Uniform sentence length.
21. Em dashes and en dashes. Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses.
22. Emojis.
23. The big reveal at the end. Put the main point first.
24. Puffed-up significance: pivotal, crucial, vital, key (adjective), testament, landscape, tapestry, journey, ecosystem, empower, elevate, leverage, robust, seamless, delve, foster, showcase, underscore, nestled, vibrant.
25. Copula avoidance: "serves as", "stands as", "boasts". Use is, are, has.
26. Tacked-on -ing phrases for fake depth ("..., ensuring peace of mind").
27. Vague authorities: "experts say", "studies show" without a named source.
28. Chatbot residue: "I hope this helps", "Great question", "Certainly!", "Let me know".
29. Excess hedging ("could potentially possibly").
30. Generic upbeat endings ("The future looks bright").
31. Staccato drama: several short fragments in a row for effect.
32. Title Case Headings. Use sentence case.
33. Bold on random phrases and bold-header bullet lists for prose that should be a sentence.

## Allowed, used with judgment

One short sentence for emphasis. A question in a FAQ heading (that is a real question). A list of three when there are exactly three real things. Contrast when it states a real trade-off. Judge clusters, not single instances.

## Page intro pattern (What, How, Why)

Hero banners and page intros answer three things in plain words: what this is, what will happen next (the steps), and why it matters to this reader. Order on the page can follow the Golden Circle (why, how, what) on marketing pages and what, how, why on working screens, where people came to do a task.

## Before publishing any copy

Read it aloud. Search for em dashes, en dashes and the banned words above. Check every fact against the product (docs/PLAN.md, contracts, pricing). For legal topics, say "may require review" and never promise an outcome.
